The biggest piece that fits both

You know the GCF of 12 and 18. What is the GCF of \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) and \(\tfrac{4}{5}\)? It is the longest piece that measures both exactly.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The GCF of 12 and 18 is 6 (see Gears, lockers and common factors). Think of it as a length: the longest stick that measures out 12 and also 18 with nothing left over. Sticks of 6 fit 2 times and 3 times.

That picture works for fractions too. For positive fractions \(x\) and \(y\), a common factor is a length \(r\) that fits into each a whole number of times. The GCF is the longest one. The word "whole" matters: any fraction fits into any other some fraction of a time, so without it every length would count.

Write \(x\) and \(y\) over the same denominator. Their GCF is the GCF of the two tops, over that denominator.

There is a shortcut. If both fractions are in lowest terms, the GCF is the GCF of the tops over the LCM of the bottoms. The lowest-terms condition is needed, as question 3 shows.

Watch one: two ribbons

You have ribbons \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) m and \(\tfrac{4}{5}\) m long. You want to cut both into pieces that are all the same length, as long as possible, with nothing wasted.

Over fifteenths: \(\tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{10}{15}\) and \(\tfrac{4}{5} = \tfrac{12}{15}\). The GCF of 10 and 12 is 2, so the GCF is \(\tfrac{2}{15}\) m. The first ribbon gives \(10 \div 2 = 5\) pieces and the second gives \(12 \div 2 = 6\).

Shortcut check: GCF(2, 4) = 2 and LCM(3, 5) = 15, so again \(\tfrac{2}{15}\).

Your turn

  1. Find the GCF of \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) and \(\tfrac{9}{10}\), and of \(\tfrac{5}{6}\) and \(\tfrac{10}{9}\). Use a common denominator, then check with the shortcut. Say how many times the GCF fits into each fraction.
  2. A floor is \(1\tfrac{1}{2}\) m by \(2\tfrac{1}{4}\) m. What is the largest square tile that covers it exactly, without cutting tiles, and how many tiles are needed?
  3. Sam wants the GCF of \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}\), but writes \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) as \(\tfrac{2}{4}\) and uses the shortcut: GCF(2, 1) over LCM(4, 3) gives \(\tfrac{1}{12}\). Find the true GCF with a common denominator. Is \(\tfrac{1}{12}\) a common factor at all? Why did the shortcut go wrong?
  4. Euclid's trick from Sharpen 6 works on fractions: replace the bigger one by its remainder after taking away as many copies of the smaller one as fit. Run it on \(\tfrac{7}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{21}{8}\). Write every line.

Stretch: two blinking lights

One light blinks every \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) second and another every \(\tfrac{5}{6}\) second. They blink together right now. When do they next blink together? That time is the LCM of the two fractions: the shortest length that each fits into a whole number of times. Guess a shortcut for the LCM of two fractions in lowest terms, and test it here. Then check whether GCF × LCM equals \(\tfrac{3}{4} \times \tfrac{5}{6}\), as it does for whole numbers.

Answers

  1. \(\tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{15}{20}\) and \(\tfrac{9}{10} = \tfrac{18}{20}\). GCF(15, 18) = 3, so the GCF is \(\tfrac{3}{20}\). Shortcut: GCF(3, 9) = 3 over LCM(4, 10) = 20. It fits 5 times into \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) and 6 times into \(\tfrac{9}{10}\). Next, \(\tfrac{5}{6} = \tfrac{15}{18}\) and \(\tfrac{10}{9} = \tfrac{20}{18}\). GCF(15, 20) = 5, so the GCF is \(\tfrac{5}{18}\). Shortcut: GCF(5, 10) = 5 over LCM(6, 9) = 18. It fits 3 times and 4 times.
  2. \(1\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{6}{4}\) and \(2\tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{9}{4}\). GCF(6, 9) = 3, so the tiles are \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) m on a side. They fit 2 times along one wall and 3 times along the other: \(2 \times 3 = 6\) tiles.
  3. \(\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{6}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2}{6}\). GCF(3, 2) = 1, so the true GCF is \(\tfrac{1}{6}\). It fits 3 times and 2 times. \(\tfrac{1}{12}\) is a common factor (it fits 6 times and 4 times) but not the greatest: it is half of \(\tfrac{1}{6}\). Writing \(\tfrac{2}{4}\) hid a factor of 2 shared by the top and bottom. The common-denominator method works with any shared denominator. The shortcut needs both fractions in lowest terms.
  4. \(\tfrac{7}{2} = \tfrac{28}{8}\). Take away one \(\tfrac{21}{8}\): \(\tfrac{28}{8} = 1 \times \tfrac{21}{8} + \tfrac{7}{8}\). Now use \(\tfrac{21}{8}\) and \(\tfrac{7}{8}\): \(\tfrac{21}{8} = 3 \times \tfrac{7}{8} + 0\). Stop. The GCF is \(\tfrac{7}{8}\). Shortcut check: GCF(7, 21) = 7 over LCM(2, 8) = 8. It works for the same reason as before: a length that fits a whole number of times into two lengths also fits a whole number of times into their difference.

Stretch. Over twelfths, \(\tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{9}{12}\) and \(\tfrac{5}{6} = \tfrac{10}{12}\). The LCM of 9 and 10 is 90, so the LCM is \(\tfrac{90}{12} = \tfrac{15}{2} = 7\tfrac{1}{2}\) seconds: 10 blinks of the first light and 9 of the second. The shortcut is LCM of the tops over GCF of the bottoms: LCM(3, 5) = 15 over GCF(4, 6) = 2. The GCF is \(\tfrac{1}{12}\), and \(\tfrac{1}{12} \times \tfrac{15}{2} = \tfrac{15}{24} = \tfrac{3}{4} \times \tfrac{5}{6}\). The product fact still holds.
